Daily flow effects, scenarios, and equilibrium

BigHole.Melrose

Read this first

No flow signal survives validation at this site. Curves and tables below are correct arithmetic on a relationship that is not detectable in this record. They are retained so the pipeline can be audited end to end, not so the numbers can be quoted. Every section is labelled accordingly.

This is a result, not a failure. It says that ~40 years of annual data cannot resolve a flow effect on these vital rates at this site, and that releases cannot be timed to trout on this evidence.

1. What was estimated

For every day of the calendar year, two curves per site:

  • β_R(d) — effect of a wetter-than-normal day on recruits per unit spawning stock
  • β_S(d) — effect on adult survival, on the logit scale

and one derived curve:

  • s(d) — the benefit per unit of water actually released, which is β(d) divided by baseline discharge and is therefore a different shape
Validation. Blocked cross-validation is the honest number; in-sample is arithmetic, not evidence. Leave-one-out is shown so the inflation from flow autocorrelation is visible.

1.1 The fitted curves

β(d) is the effect on the response of one standard deviation of extra flow on day d, holding every other day fixed. It is the primary estimate — everything in §3 and §4 is a summary of it.

Read it against its band, not as a line. Where the interval spans zero the data cannot tell the sign of the effect on that day, however sharp the peak looks.

At BigHole.Melrose, β(d) is largest on 06 Jun and most negative on 11 Apr. Its 95% interval excludes zero on 11% of days.

The survival curve is fitted at a lag of 1/0 year(s), chosen by the sweep in script 04 and gated in script 05. That lag is fitted independently of the recruitment flow lag, so the two curves need not describe the same year.

3. Guidance for water managers (RQ1)

3.1 The two curves are not the same shape

This is the practical core. β(d) says when the population is sensitive to hydrology; s(d) says when a unit of released water buys the most fish. They differ because the same water is a large proportional change at base flow and a small one at the snowmelt peak.

3.2 How precisely can a day be named?

The honest resolution. Each simulated curve votes for its best and worst day; this is the credible interval of that vote. Width is a property of the record, not a modelling choice.
Process Which Window Median Width (days) Credible level Resolvable

8 recruitment most harmful day 11 Apr – 09 Oct 24 Aug 181 80% no — report a season
19 survival most beneficial day 30 Jan – 18 Mar 30 Jan 47 80% yes
20 survival most harmful day 24 Aug – 22 Dec 06 Sep 120 80% no — report a season

At least one window exceeds 90 days. For those, no date should be reported anywhere in the manuscript or in guidance to managers — the record supports a season, not a week. Stating a date would give actionable-looking advice built on noise, which is worse than declining to advise, because water gets moved and money gets spent.

3.4 The season when water can actually be released

Sections 3.1–3.3 use the full calendar year, which is the right ecological domain — β(d) is estimated over all 365 days and should be reported that way. But nobody can release water in February. Leased water, shepherded water and reservoir re-timing are negotiated and delivered across the irrigation season.

Restricting to that season is a constrained optimisation, not a zoom. The best feasible day is an argmax over a subset and can be a different day; its credible interval has to be recomputed within the subset rather than truncated; and release schedules must be restricted to starts whose full duration fits inside the season.

The window used here is the irrigation and allocation season: 01 May to 31 Oct, 184 days. Set it in config.R under CFG$decision_window.

The curve across the season, and what drives it

Baseline discharge is on the right axis for a reason. Benefit per cfs scales as 1/Q, and discharge falls sharply from the snowmelt peak to autumn base flow — so the same acre-foot buys progressively more as the season goes on. Where the benefit curve peaks later than β(d) does, that gap is pure 1/Q leverage, and it is the part a water user can act on.

The best feasible day, and what the constraint costs

What the allocation season costs. ‘Benefit retained’ is the effect at the best feasible day divided by the effect at the unconstrained optimum. ‘Constraint binds’ is how often the globally best day fell outside the season.
Process Site Best day (unconstrained) Best feasible day Feasible window Width (days) Benefit retained Constraint binds Resolvable
4 recruitment BigHole.Melrose 20 Mar 08 Aug 26 Jun – 30 Oct 126 44% 88% of draws no — report a period
10 survival BigHole.Melrose 30 Jan 16 Jul 15 Jul – 13 Aug 29 30% 97% of draws yes

The biology wants water when the allocation system cannot deliver it. The best feasible day retains only 30% of the unconstrained optimum. This is a finding, not a nuisance: it says the binding constraint on trout benefit here is not how much water is available for lease but when it can be moved. That conclusion belongs in the abstract, and it points at a different intervention — changing the rules about when water can be re-timed — rather than at buying more of it.

Does the timing decision actually matter?

Across schedules, a median of 6% of feasible start dates land within 10% of the best achievable benefit.

Timing is the binding decision. Only a narrow part of the season is near-optimal, so when the water moves matters as much as how much of it there is. This is the case where negotiating for a specific delivery window is worth the transaction cost — provided the interval in the table above is narrow enough to name one.

Which days drive each scenario?

A scenario can act because flow changes on days the population is sensitive to, or because it changes greatly on days it is not. The multiplier alone cannot tell those apart, and they call for different management responses.

4b. Equilibrium carrying capacity (rule-curve arm)

A separate analysis answering a different question. Sections 3 and 4 ask when water helps and what a scenario does over a 30-year projection. This asks: if the summer mean flow settles at a new level, where does the population settle?

Two differences from everything above, both deliberate. It reads the flow-informed IPM, because it propagates the flow response that model already fitted rather than estimating a new one. And it uses its own standardization — the z of the annual summer mean, on exactly the scale the IPM was fitted with.

6. Limitations

  • Rearing-year effect. The flow lag (2) is shorter than the recruit lag (3), so the hydrograph is the age-0 rearing year, not the spawning year. Spawning-year conditions are not represented, and β_R(d) should not be read as a spawning effect.

  • Incubation spans the year boundary. On a calendar axis, December of the spawning year and January–March of the rearing year fall in different lag years. A mechanism operating continuously through incubation is split between them and will read weakly in both (docs/notes/CALENDAR_AXIS.md).

  • Growth is frozen. Mean mass at age is held at its launch value, so production results capture the flow-on-abundance pathway only, not temperature-mediated growth.

  • Local linearity. Effects are exact under the fitted model but the model is linear in the standardized anomaly, so large extrapolations beyond the observed flow range are least reliable — which is where the wettest and driest scenarios sit.

  • Resolution. Where the best-day interval exceeds 90 days, guidance is seasonal. This is a limit of a ~40-year annual record, not of the method.

  • Stock enters twice. log(R/S) contains −log(S) and S is also the density predictor, so shared posterior uncertainty biases the density coefficient downward (Walters & Ludwig 1981). The flow curves are affected only through the correlation between stock and the flow predictors, which is reported by script 03.
