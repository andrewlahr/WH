Daily streamflow and brown trout population processes

Materials and Methods

Study system

Brown trout (Salmo trutta) populations in regulated Montana rivers, with the Madison River below Ennis Lake (USGS gage 06041000) as the focal site. Discharge is managed under Bureau of Reclamation operations. Analysis covers 33 recruit-years.

Population model and the response variables

Abundance and demographic rates were estimated with a Bayesian age-structured integrated population model fitted in JAGS. Mark–recapture abundance estimation is conducted separately, under a protocol agreed with Montana Fish, Wildlife and Parks so that estimates match published values. The model tracks age-2 recruits (\(N_2\)), age-3 (\(N_3\)), and an age-4-plus group (\(N_4\)), with Ricker recruitment on adult biomass

\[\log R_t = \alpha_0 + \log B_{t-\ell} - b\,B_{t-\ell} - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_R^2 + \varepsilon_t\]

where \(B_t = N_{3,t}w_3 + N_{4,t}w_4\) is standing adult biomass in grams and \(\ell = 3\) years, and density-dependent apparent survival

\[\mathrm{logit}(s_t) = \phi_0 + \phi_N\,N_{\mathrm{all},t-1}/100 + \zeta_t .\]

The posterior used here contains no flow covariate. This is the circularity guard: were abundance estimated under a flow-informed model, a flow signal would be embedded in the response before any regression was fitted.

Two responses were constructed, each on all 20000 retained posterior draws so that abundance uncertainty propagates rather than being collapsed to a point estimate:

Recruitment. \(y_t^{(m)} = \log R_t^{(m)} - \log B_{t-\ell}^{(m)}\). Stock is adult biomass, matching the IPM’s own Ricker.

Survival. The process deviate \(\varepsilon_t^{(m)} = \mathrm{logit}(s_t^{(m)}) - \phi_0^{(m)} - \phi_N^{(m)} N_{\mathrm{all},t-1}^{(m)}/100\), i.e. the part of survival the density-dependent null model leaves unexplained. Regressing survival itself on discharge would re-attribute density-driven variation to flow, because flow drives recruitment and recruitment drives density at a lag.

Hydrograph standardization

Daily discharge was log-transformed, centred on a day-of-year climatology, and scaled by a single global standard deviation:

\[x_y(t) = \frac{\log Q_y(t) - \bar{c}(t)}{\sigma},\qquad \bar{c}(t) = \frac{1}{n_y}\sum_y \log Q_y(t).\]

The scale is global rather than day-specific; a per-day scale would inflate low-variance winter days relative to the snowmelt peak. Curves are placed on a calendar-year axis (1 January – 31 December). This axis was chosen for interpretability: results are reported to water managers, irrigators and lease-holders who work in calendar years, and a day index anchored to 1 October is a persistent source of confusion outside the hydrologic community.

The trade-off is stated plainly. Brown trout spawn in autumn and emerge the following spring, so the spawning-to-emergence interval straddles the calendar boundary and no single calendar year contains all of it. The analysis accommodates this through the site-specific flow lag rather than through the axis: a lag equal to the recruit lag places the hydrograph in the spawning year, while a shorter lag places it in the rearing year, and the integrated population model selected between these hypotheses independently at each site. A consequence is that a mechanism operating continuously across the turn of the year — incubation from December through March — is split between two lag years and would be detected only weakly in either. Years without a complete 365-day record were excluded.

Functional regression

For each posterior draw the response was regressed on the full daily hydrograph,

\[y_t^{(m)} = \mu^{(m)} + \gamma^{(m)} S_{t-\ell} + \sum_{d=1}^{365}\beta^{(m)}(d)\,x_{t}(d) + e_t^{(m)},\]

with \(\beta(\cdot)\) expanded in the leading functional principal components of the standardized hydrographs. Functional PCA was computed on the training curves, components were retained until they jointly explained 90% of hydrograph variance, and only those individually explaining at least 1%, subject to a maximum of 6. The response was regressed on the retained component scores by ordinary least squares and the coefficients mapped back to the daily domain as \(\beta(d) = \sum_k b_k \phi_k(d)\). At BigHole.Melrose this retained 6 components, spanning 79.2% of hydrograph variance.

The component cap, rather than the variance rule, determined \(K\) at this site; the reported \(K\) should therefore be read as a ceiling imposed by the analyst rather than a property of the data.

Component selection is unsupervised with respect to the response: components are ordered by variance in the predictor and only then tested against recruitment. A mode of flow variation carrying little variance but a strong biological signal would be discarded before it was tested (Jolliffe 1982). We therefore report the incremental predictive contribution of the discarded components in SI, and note that the covariance of \(\hat\beta\) is of rank \(K\) and so excludes uncertainty in the truncation itself.

The hydrograph paired with each recruit year is not assumed to be the spawning year. The integrated population model’s indicator-variable selection chose a flow lag independently at each site, and that choice is carried through here: a lag equal to the recruit lag identifies spawning-year conditions, while a shorter lag identifies age-0 rearing-year conditions. On a calendar axis these are distinct years and the mapping is direct: the hydrograph for recruit year \(t\) is that of calendar year \(t - L\), where \(L\) is the selected lag. The selected lag is reported per site rather than assumed (derived_stats.csv, flow_lag_*).

The full 365 × 365 covariance of \(\hat\beta\) was retained. Adjacent days are strongly correlated, so every statement about where the curve is high — bands, contrasts, the location of extrema — requires the off-diagonal terms.

Survival lags of 0 and 1 years were fitted separately, because adult survival responds to contemporaneous or prior-year conditions rather than to conditions three years earlier; the selected lag was 1 and its permutation \(p\)-value is Bonferroni-adjusted for the number of lags examined.

A pre-registered confirmatory model

Because a 365-point curve estimated from 33 annual observations spends degrees of freedom on structure the data may not support, the same responses were also regressed on the mean standardized anomaly within four pre-registered calendar-year windows — autumn spawning, overwinter incubation, spring snowmelt, summer base flow (1–90; 91–181; 182–273; 274–365 on the day of year). This four-parameter model is the powered confirmatory test of whether flow matters at all; \(\beta(d)\) then describes when. Both were scored on identical folds against identical nulls.

Validation

Predictive skill was assessed by blocked cross-validation holding out contiguous 5-year runs. Leave-one-year-out validation is inappropriate here: interannual flow is autocorrelated, so a held-out hydrograph is closely approximated by retained neighbours and the fold measures interpolation rather than prediction. Both are reported so the magnitude of that inflation is visible.

Because the null sampling distribution of cross-validated \(R^2\) at this sample size is neither centred at zero nor symmetric, skill was referred to a permutation null formed by circularly shifting the response against the hydrograph series, preserving the autocorrelation of both series and breaking only their alignment. Random shuffling would destroy the autocorrelation and return anticonservative \(p\)-values. With 33 years the finest resolvable \(p\)-value is approximately 0.030.

Neither specification improved on the series mean out of sample. We report the null: seasonal hydrograph structure has no detectable effect on the vital rates at this site over the available record. Curves are presented for completeness and are labelled unsupported.

From β(d) to management guidance

β(d) is the effect of a one-standard-deviation standardized log anomaly. Water is released in cfs. Adding \(\Delta Q\) on day \(d\) changes the flow signal by

\[\Delta\eta(d) = \frac{\beta(d)}{\sigma}\log\!\left(1 + \frac{\Delta Q}{\bar{Q}(d)}\right),\]

where \(\bar{Q}(d)\) is the geometric-mean baseline discharge on that day, so the benefit per unit of water is approximately \(\beta(d)/[\sigma\bar{Q}(d)]\). Because baseline discharge spans more than an order of magnitude across the calendar year, this reshapes the curve: identical water is a large proportional change at base flow and a negligible one at the snowmelt peak. We therefore distinguish throughout between β(d), the ecological sensitivity, and \(s(d)\), the management sensitivity. The logarithm also encodes diminishing returns, so the model implies that distributing a fixed volume over a longer window outperforms a short pulse.

For a leased volume \(V\) released evenly over a window \(W\), total benefit is \(\sigma^{-1}\sum_{d\in W}\beta(d)\log(1 + \kappa V/[|W|\bar Q(d)])\), maximised over window start and length.

The unconstrained optimum is of limited operational use, because water cannot be moved at arbitrary times of year. We therefore repeated the optimisation restricted to the season in which allocation decisions are actually made (irrigation and allocation season, 01 May to 31 Oct). This is a constrained optimisation rather than a restricted plot: the best feasible day is an argmax over a subset and may differ from the global optimum; its credible interval is recomputed within the subset rather than truncated, because a simulation whose global peak falls outside the season relocates to its best feasible day rather than dropping out; and only release windows lying wholly inside the season are admitted.

We report the resulting cost of the constraint — the benefit at the best feasible day as a fraction of the benefit at the unconstrained optimum. A low value indicates that the biologically most valuable timing lies outside any window the allocation system can reach, which is a statement about institutional timing rather than about water volume.

Resolution

The location of a maximum was not read from the fitted curve. Whole curves were simulated 10000 times from the combined distribution — a posterior draw for abundance uncertainty plus a multivariate normal deviate using the full covariance for regression uncertainty — and the day maximising \(s(d)\) recorded for each. The 80% interval of that distribution is reported as the resolvable window. Its width is a property of the record rather than a modelling choice. Simultaneous confidence bands were obtained from the same simulations as the 95th percentile of the maximum standardized deviation, giving a critical value of 3.18 rather than 1.96 (Ruppert, Wand & Carroll 2003 §6.5).

Total production

Production is a consequence of recruitment and survival rather than a directly observed response, so it was not regressed on flow. Instead, flow perturbations were propagated through the age-structured model. Total biomass follows the exact identity \(B_t = B_{t-1} + I_t + G_t - M_t\), partitioning annual change into recruitment input, somatic growth, and mortality loss, with the age-4-plus group valued at its abundance-weighted mean mass. There is no harvest term in this model, so surplus production equals the change in biomass. The identity residual is computed for every draw and year and is zero to machine precision by construction.

Sensitivity was computed as a paired numerical derivative: for each 30-day block the projection was run with and without a 25 cfs release under common random numbers, so the difference reflects the release rather than Monte Carlo noise. Runs with each pathway enabled separately, and both together, isolate the interaction — the compounding of a survival gain into future spawning stock, which summing the two curves would miss. Mean mass at age is held at its launch value, so results capture the flow-on-abundance pathway and not temperature-mediated changes in growth.

Scenario projections

Bureau of Reclamation downscaled daily discharge was standardized against the training climatology and scale, so projected and observed hydrographs share an axis; standardizing each scenario against itself would re-centre a uniformly drier future to appear average and eliminate the effect. Scenario effects are \(\eta = \sum_d \beta(d)[x_{\text{scen}}(d) - \bar{x}_{\text{hist}}(d)]\), and are additionally decomposed by day to identify which days drive a scenario’s effect — a scenario may act because flow changes on days the population is sensitive to, or because it changes greatly on days it is not. The fraction of scenario days falling outside the training range is reported per scenario.

Equilibrium carrying capacity under sustained flow change

The preceding analyses treat flow as a perturbation and ask when it is most valuable. A separate question, and the one most often asked of reservoir operations, is where the population settles if summer flow shifts and stays shifted. We addressed this with a rule-curve analysis, run as an independent arm.

Recruitment and survival were evaluated on a stable-age mapping from spawning stock to standing abundance, \(X = S/[s(1-s)]\) and \(\mathrm{SSB} = S\,w(s)\), giving a closed-form replacement curve

\[Y(X) = \frac{\exp\!\left(\alpha_0 + \log \mathrm{SSB} - b\,\mathrm{SSB} + \beta_1 z + \beta_2 z^2\right)}{1 - \mathrm{logit}^{-1}\!\left(\phi_0 + \phi_1 z + \phi_2 z^2 + \phi_N X/100\right)},\]

where \(z\) is the standardized mean summer discharge. Because survival does not depend on recruitment, \(Y\) is available in closed form and needs no simulation. Carrying capacity \(K\) is the descending intersection of \(Y(X)\) with the replacement line \(Y = X\), located by linear interpolation; maximum surplus production is \(\max_X\,[Y(X) - X]\), refined parabolically. Both were computed for every posterior draw on a common deterministic grid, so that a scenario contrast reflects flow rather than resampling.

Two features of this arm differ from the rest of the analysis and are deliberate. First, it uses the flow-informed integrated population model rather than the null fit, because it propagates the flow response that model estimated rather than estimating a new one; the circularity concern that motivates the null fit elsewhere does not arise. Second, its covariate is the standardized annual mean summer discharge on the scale the integrated model was fitted with, verified to reproduce that model’s own covariate to within \(10^{-10}\); the daily standardization used for \(\beta(d)\) would place the recruitment and survival coefficients off-scale.

We report the fraction of scenario-years falling outside the calibration range of \(z\), and the location of the vertex of each quadratic flow response. A vertex inside the observed range with downward curvature implies that the fitted response already peaks within the historical record.

[VERIFY before submission: the stable-age weighting \(w(s)\) has two conventions in the source implementation — standing spawning biomass, \(w = w_3 + [s/(1-s)]w_4\), and the alternative that weights deaths, \(w = (1-s)w_3 + s\,w_4\). The analysis selects between them by which reproduces the posterior’s own biomass series, not by preference. State which was used and the agreement obtained.]
